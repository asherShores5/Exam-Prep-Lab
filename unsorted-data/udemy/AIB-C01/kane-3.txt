Question 1Skipped

A regional theater network's finance team must produce monthly invoices for venue rentals. The amount due is fully defined by current rate cards, rental hours, tax tables, and signed discounts. Executives ask whether the team should use AI because a no-code ML tool could learn from prior invoices. The finance team needs repeatable totals that auditors can trace to source rules.

Which approach should the team recommend?
Use SageMaker Canvas to train a no-code model that predicts invoice totals from historical billing records.
Explanation
SageMaker Canvas is a no-code ML capability, but it is still an ML approach that learns from data and evaluates model outputs. Learning from past invoices does not provide the same traceability as applying current billing rules directly.
Use a custom ML model to learn billing patterns and refresh predictions after each billing cycle.
Explanation
A custom ML model is useful when a team needs flexibility for prediction problems that cannot be captured reliably with simple rules. In this scenario, the invoice total is not uncertain, so a learned prediction would add avoidable error risk.
Use ML predictions for first-pass totals and let customers dispute any invoice that appears incorrect.
Explanation
This is a reactive approach that shifts error detection to customers after invoices are sent. It does not meet the need for repeatable, auditable totals before billing occurs.
Correct answer
Use rule-based automation to calculate invoice totals from approved tax tables and contract terms.
Explanation
This approach fits a process in which the correct answer is determined by known rules and calculations. It provides repeatable results that auditors can trace to the rate cards, tax tables, and contract terms.
Overall explanation

When an outcome can be determined by explicit steps, formulas, or business rules, rule-based automation is the better fit. AWS guidance on when to use machine learning states that ML is not needed if the target value can be determined with programmable rules or computations without data-driven learning. In this scenario, the invoice total comes from rate cards, tax tables, rental hours, and contract discounts, so ordinary automation can produce the required result directly.

ML is most useful when rules are difficult to write reliably, when many overlapping factors affect the answer, or when a human judgment process needs to scale. It is also important to remember that ML systems learn from observations and commonly provide probabilistic predictions rather than the same deterministic behavior expected from traditional software. A no-code ML tool can reduce technical effort, but no-code does not change the fact that the approach is still ML and must be evaluated as a learned model. For more detail, see When to use machine learning and What is machine learning?.

Question 2Skipped

An agricultural cooperative's crop advisory team is planning an AI assistant that will suggest pest control and irrigation actions to member farms. The team has already listed business goals for faster agronomist responses and broader after-hours support. Before approving a pilot, the sponsor wants governance by design so that expected benefits, foreseeable risks, and organizational obligations are considered during planning.

Which planning action should the sponsor require?
Correct answer
Define release criteria that include safety, privacy, fairness, explainability, and transparency for the crop advice use case.
Explanation
This approach integrates responsible AI considerations into planning before the pilot proceeds. Release criteria translate expected benefits, risks, and obligations into readiness expectations for a specific use case, not just general product goals.
Use AWS AI Service Cards as the sole release approval record, then skip testing with cooperative specific inputs before farm rollout.
Explanation
AWS AI Service Cards can help teams understand intended uses, limitations, and responsible AI design choices for AWS AI services. They do not replace the organization's responsibility to evaluate the system for its own use case, users, inputs, and obligations.
Defer responsible AI review until farmers submit beta feedback and the team confirms pilot adoption and support targets.
Explanation
This approach treats responsible AI as a late review activity after users have already interacted with the system. Governance by design requires teams to consider risks, obligations, and affected stakeholders during planning, not only after adoption signals appear.
Limit readiness criteria to usability, response time, and uptime, then add responsible AI guardrails during operations.
Explanation
Usability and service performance can be important, but they do not cover the responsible AI factors needed for release decisions. Adding guardrails later can miss design-stage choices that should be built into the use case, workflow, and evaluation plan.
Overall explanation

Governance by design means responsible AI work is part of planning, design, and operations rather than a separate approval step after the solution is built. In the AWS Well-Architected Responsible AI Lens, release criteria are planning artifacts that connect expected benefits, risks, and organizational obligations to readiness expectations for the AI system and related datasets.

For a crop advice assistant, readiness should not be measured only by adoption, usability, or response time. Responsible AI criteria can include factors such as safety, privacy, robustness, fairness, explainability, transparency, and related concerns that matter for the specific use case. The Lens also emphasizes that responsible AI practices should be embedded throughout the lifecycle, with issues identified and addressed during design where changes are usually easier and less disruptive.

AWS AI Service Cards are useful transparency resources, but they do not prove that a particular deployment is ready for a particular organization's users, inputs, and obligations. They can inform planning, while the cooperative still needs its own use case specific evaluation and release criteria. For more information, see Release criteria, Design principles, and Responsible AI resources.

Question 3Skipped

A medical device manufacturer's service operations group is replacing a rules-based field technician knowledge portal with a generative AI assistant. Manuals, service bulletins, and troubleshooting guides change often. Technicians also report that answer usefulness varies across product lines. Leaders want to prepare the transition without assuming that a new AI platform will automatically provide current, reliable guidance.

Which TWO actions should the group include in the transition plan?
Prioritize fine-tuning alone to replace ongoing content retrieval and review.
Explanation
Fine-tuning can be useful for some domain-specific needs, but using it alone is not a substitute for current content retrieval and human feedback. Frequently changing service guidance requires a plan to keep context current and evaluate answer quality over time.
Delay user review until after full rollout to avoid slowing the migration schedule.
Explanation
Postponing user review increases the risk that the assistant will provide irrelevant or unreliable guidance at scale. Transition planning should include validation and feedback before broad adoption, especially for processes that affect field service outcomes.
Move all available documents before assessing content quality, ownership, and access.
Explanation
Bulk migration without assessing quality, ownership, and access is a weak data-readiness approach. Generative AI systems can amplify problems from outdated, inconsistent, or poorly governed content.
Correct selection
Create feedback loops for technicians and experts to review answer relevance over time.
Explanation
Feedback loops help the organization evaluate whether responses remain useful, accurate, and relevant across product lines. They also support continuous improvement after the transition, rather than treating launch as the end of readiness work.
Correct selection
Evaluate a RAG approach that retrieves approved, current service content for responses.
Explanation
Retrieval Augmented Generation can help a generative AI assistant use current enterprise content as context instead of relying only on the model's general training. This is especially relevant when manuals and bulletins change frequently and must remain aligned with approved guidance.
Overall explanation

When an organization transitions a business process to generative AI, the plan should treat data strategy as an ongoing operating concern, not as a one-time upload of documents. AWS guidance identifies Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG), data preparation, fine-tuning, and continuous feedback loops as choices that can improve model performance and relevance when they are matched to the use case.

For a knowledge assistant that depends on frequently updated manuals and bulletins, RAG is valuable because it can provide the model with current, approved content at response time. Fine-tuning can help adapt behavior or domain language in some cases, but it does not by itself solve the problem of changing source material or remove the need for review.

Feedback loops are also important because answer quality can vary across products, regions, or user groups. Technicians and subject matter experts can help identify gaps, stale content, and relevance issues so the organization can improve the process after launch. See AWS guidance on data strategy for generative AI in Data considerations for generative AI and the staged adoption journey in Generative AI adoption journey.

Question 4Skipped

A continuing education provider uses a generative AI tutor to help adult learners practice course concepts. The tutor met launch goals for availability and response speed. Over time, course content changes, learner questions shift, and instructors report that some answers are less useful even though the service remains available.

Which TWO actions should the provider take to manage model drift and performance changes after launch?
Correct selection
Define alerts and response steps for drift, quality, and risk signals.
Explanation
This action is correct because monitoring should lead to timely action when quality or risk indicators change. Alerts and response steps help teams detect issues and make updates before degraded results become a sustained business problem.
Freeze prompts and course sources after the initial launch approval.
Explanation
This action is incorrect because course content and learner needs can change after launch. Freezing prompts and sources can prevent the tutor from staying aligned with current materials and user expectations.
Schedule a yearly refresh without interim monitoring or feedback signals.
Explanation
This action is incorrect because drift and performance changes can emerge between scheduled refreshes. Waiting for a fixed annual update can allow inaccurate, irrelevant, or risky responses to persist.
Review only uptime, latency, and error rates after the tutor is released.
Explanation
This action is incorrect because uptime and latency show whether the application is operating, not whether the AI output is still accurate or relevant. A solution can meet system health targets while model quality or user satisfaction declines.
Correct selection
Track user interaction and model quality signals alongside system health.
Explanation
This action is correct because an AI solution can remain technically available while answer relevance, usefulness, or risk changes over time. Monitoring must include user and model quality signals, not only application availability and speed.
Overall explanation

AI solutions require ongoing monitoring because the environment around the solution changes after deployment. New course content, new user behavior, and changing expectations can reduce answer relevance even when the application remains fast and available.

A strong monitoring plan looks beyond infrastructure. AWS describes production monitoring for generative AI across application and system health, business and user-interaction health, and model and AI quality health, including drift detection, accuracy or relevance, bias or fairness, and traceability. See Production monitoring and performance for generative AI applications.

The practical goal is not just to create dashboards. Monitoring should support capture, alerts, and response so that teams can investigate degraded quality, collect feedback, and make iterative changes to improve accuracy, quality, and relevance over time. Limiting review to uptime, freezing prompts, or waiting for a yearly refresh treats AI as a one-time launch rather than a system that needs continuous improvement.

Question 5Skipped

An international disaster relief nonprofit is expanding an AI-assisted triage tool that prioritizes incoming aid requests from text messages after storms, floods, and earthquakes. The pilot worked well in one country, but the expanded program will cover new regions, languages, and emergency conditions. Incorrect triage could delay help for urgent requests.

Which TWO actions should the nonprofit take to reduce AI system reliability risk before broad operational use?
Correct selection
Test outputs with representative regional scenarios and edge cases before expanding the triage system.
Explanation
This action is correct because reliability should be assessed against the conditions in which the system will actually be used. Representative scenarios and edge cases help reveal failures that a narrow pilot may not expose, especially when geography, language, and emergency type change.
Reuse the original pilot setup for every region to keep triage decisions operationally consistent.
Explanation
This action is incorrect because consistency of setup does not ensure reliability across new regions and emergency conditions. A pilot configuration that worked in one country might perform poorly when language, terminology, or incident patterns differ.
Measure adoption by message volume and agent usage as the primary indicator of system reliability.
Explanation
This action is incorrect because usage metrics show whether people are using the system, not whether the system is producing reliable triage decisions. Reliability requires evidence that outputs remain accurate, robust, and appropriate under real operating conditions.
Automate all triage decisions during disasters and review aggregate outcomes after the season.
Explanation
This action is incorrect because it allows unreliable recommendations to affect urgent requests before issues are detected. Reviewing outcomes only after the season is a reactive anti-pattern for a high-impact operational process.
Correct selection
Retain human review and fallback procedures for uncertain or high-impact triage recommendations.
Explanation
This action is correct because high-impact AI recommendations should not be treated as fully reliable when uncertainty or potential harm is high. Human review and fallback procedures reduce the risk that an unreliable output directly delays urgent aid.
Overall explanation

AI reliability risk increases when a system moves from a controlled pilot into new operating conditions. The nonprofit should use representative validation, including regional examples and edge cases, because a system that performed well in one context can fail when language, user behavior, or emergency patterns change.

For high-impact decisions, reliability is not only a model-quality issue. It is also an operating-model issue. Keeping human review and fallback procedures for uncertain or consequential recommendations limits the chance that an unreliable output directly causes harm while the organization learns how the system performs at scale.

Freezing the pilot setup treats consistency as if it were reliability, but unchanged settings can produce inconsistent real-world outcomes when conditions differ. Fully automating triage and waiting for a later review is especially risky because it delays detection until after affected people may have experienced harm. Adoption metrics can support change management, but they are not a substitute for outcome-based reliability evaluation.

The AWS Well-Architected guidance for machine learning emphasizes designing workloads to handle change, failure, and operational uncertainty rather than assuming that pilot performance will generalize automatically. See the Reliability pillar in the Machine Learning Lens for AWS guidance on reliability considerations for ML workloads.

Question 6Skipped

A municipal tax office is considering a generative AI assistant that would help contact center agents answer resident questions about local tax rules. The sponsor has seen a polished vendor demo and must decide whether the project is ready for a funded proof of concept. The rules change frequently, and some example inquiries include personal tax information.

Which action would provide the BEST leading indicator that the AI project can succeed?
Broaden the demo audience to collect agent reactions, count usage sessions, and prioritize interface changes first.
Explanation
Agent reactions and usage counts can help assess adoption, but they do not show whether the assistant can answer accurately or safely. A popular demo can still fail if source information is stale, evaluation data is weak, or sensitive data is handled without proper approval.
Move quickly to live resident records, measure answer speed, and resolve privacy questions after the pilot period.
Explanation
Using live sensitive records before privacy review is an anti-pattern for a proof of concept. AWS guidance says synthetic or fully anonymized data should be the default, and real sensitive data requires approval from information security and legal teams before ingestion.
Correct answer
Assess data readiness with approved anonymized test content, reference answers, and current policy sources.
Explanation
This action tests whether the assistant has the trustworthy inputs and benchmarks needed for a meaningful proof of concept. For a generative AI PoC, data readiness includes current and relevant source content, appropriate handling of sensitive information, and validated references for evaluating output quality.
Start fine-tuning immediately on archived policy manuals, compare response phrasing, and plan content updates later.
Explanation
Fine-tuning on archived material does not address the need for current, authoritative policy sources. Comparing phrasing quality also misses a key success indicator: whether answers are grounded, accurate, and evaluated against trusted references.
Overall explanation

A polished demo is not enough evidence that a generative AI initiative is ready for investment. AWS Prescriptive Guidance describes a successful proof of concept as one that validates business value, data readiness, technical feasibility, and risk mitigation, so the decision is based on evidence rather than enthusiasm. See Architecting a successful generative AI proof of concept.

In this scenario, the strongest leading indicator is whether the team can use appropriate test content, current policy sources, and validated reference answers to evaluate the assistant. For generative AI, high-quality evaluation data and ground-truth references are especially important because the team is assessing generated outputs, not merely training a traditional model.

The privacy constraint also matters. AWS guidance says the default for a PoC should be synthetic or fully anonymized data, and real sensitive data or personally identifiable information should be used only with explicit approval from information security and legal teams. Usage reactions, latency, or response style can be useful later, but they cannot compensate for weak source data, stale references, or unresolved data-risk issues.

Question 7Skipped

An industrial equipment manufacturer's AI governance council is reviewing AI proposals from sales, service, and workforce planning. Each business team uses different labels for risk, such as acceptable, watch list, and executive review. Leaders cannot compare which proposals need governance attention first. The organization already uses a common enterprise framework to compare operational and compliance risks.

Which action should the council take to classify AI risk consistently across the proposals?
Rank proposals by expected business value first. Add governance controls after funding is approved.
Explanation
This action prioritizes financial upside before responsible AI risk classification. Governance decisions should be informed early by the likelihood and severity of potential harms, not deferred until after investment decisions are made.
Adopt one vendor audit framework as the standard. Assign equal ratings to proposals that use similar tools.
Explanation
This action confuses audit preparation with use-case risk classification. Similar tools can create different risks depending on the stakeholder group, context, potential harms, and intended business use.
Let each team keep its risk labels. Normalize the labels during the quarterly portfolio review.
Explanation
This action allows inconsistent scoring to continue during the assessment itself. Normalizing labels later can obscure how teams judged harms and can make prioritization less reliable across the AI lifecycle.
Correct answer
Use the existing enterprise framework before assessments. Rate AI harms with a shared likelihood and severity scale.
Explanation
This action establishes a consistent methodology before teams classify risks. AWS guidance recommends using an existing organizational methodology when available and mapping potential harms by likelihood and severity so use cases can be compared.
Overall explanation

For AI governance, a useful risk classification process must create comparable ratings across business units. The AWS Responsible AI Lens recommends establishing the risk methodology before assessment and using an existing organizational methodology when available, so teams can compare AI risks across use cases in a consistent way. See Assess risks.

The assessment should estimate likelihood and severity for potential harmful interactions, then map those estimates to a risk level. A shared matrix helps leaders prioritize mitigation investments toward the highest-risk harms while still considering cost and feasibility, as described in Assign risk levels. A business-value-first or team-by-team labeling approach can make governance reactive and inconsistent. Vendor transparency and audit frameworks can support governance, but they do not replace a use-case-based risk classification method.

A digital pathology product group at a medical device manufacturer is evaluating sourcing options for an AI feature that flags possible findings for clinician review. The feature is central to the manufacturer's future product differentiation. The group has proprietary annotated image data, experienced AI scientists, a dedicated budget, and an 18-month product roadmap. Regulatory reviewers need evidence that the organization can validate model behavior and manage changes over time. A packaged vendor tool could speed up a demo, but the contract limits visibility into behavior changes and does not allow embedding the tool in the manufacturer's commercial product.

Which sourcing approach should the product group recommend?
Use a partner to adapt a general AI application, and defer validation evidence until clinical rollout.
Explanation
A partner can be useful when internal capabilities or timelines require outside help, but deferring validation evidence conflicts with the regulatory need described in the scenario. The feature is also a strategic product differentiator, so outsourcing the core capability can weaken control over product value.
Adopt the packaged vendor tool for the product, and treat vendor approval as sufficient compliance coverage.

The packaged tool does not meet the commercial embedding need and limits visibility into behavior changes. Vendor approval alone does not replace the manufacturer's need to evaluate regulatory evidence for a product feature.
Reject partner and vendor input across the portfolio, and require every AI use case to be built internally.
Explanation
Building this specific regulated product capability may be justified, but applying that rule to every AI use case is an anti-pattern. AWS guidance warns against not-invented-here thinking and recommends evaluating buy versus build as AI portfolios scale.

Choose a build approach for the regulated product, and use partners only to fill non-core capability gaps.
Explanation
This approach fits a product-differentiating AI capability when the organization has relevant data, budget, time, and internal expertise. Partner support can still help with delivery activities that are not central to the proprietary model or regulatory evidence.

Build, buy, and partner decisions should start from business value, feasibility, and risk, not from a preferred technology path. In this scenario, the AI capability is part of the manufacturer's product differentiation, the team has proprietary data and internal expertise, and regulatory reviewers need evidence over model behavior and change management. Those factors make a build approach reasonable, while still allowing selective partner help for areas that do not define the core product advantage.

See Business perspective: The AI strategy in the age of AI/ML. AWS also distinguishes different abstraction levels for generative AI options, including services for adopting higher-level capabilities, building applications with foundation model choice and customization, and using Amazon SageMaker AI for building, training, and deploying ML models at scale in appropriate cases, as described in Choosing an AWS generative AI service.

The vendor option falls short because the contract constraints conflict with the product and compliance needs. The broad internal-build-only option also falls short because it turns a justified build decision for one strategic product feature into a portfolio-wide rule, which can waste time and budget on non-differentiating use cases.

Question 9Skipped

A humanitarian aid nonprofit's strategy group is inventorying data for AI use cases. It has donation records in tables with fixed columns for donor ID, date, amount, and campaign code; grant proposal PDFs with narrative sections; and partner reports exported as JSON and XML files. The group must explain how data types affect the effort required for AI analysis.

Which guidance should the group provide?
Classify PDF grant narratives as structured data, and query them as records because proposal sections repeat consistently across files.
Explanation
Repeated headings in narrative PDFs do not by themselves create a predefined schema with rows, columns, field types, and constraints. These documents usually require extraction or preprocessing before conventional structured analysis can be applied.
Classify donation tables as semi-structured data, and analyze only metadata because their columns include dates and codes.
Explanation
Tables with fixed columns for donor ID, date, amount, and campaign code are structured data. Dates, short text, codes, and numbers are common structured data types when they are organized into consistent attributes and records.
Correct answer
Classify JSON and XML exports as semi-structured data, and assess their tags and nesting before choosing AI processing.
Explanation
JSON and XML are semi-structured because they are not organized as relational tables but include self-describing elements such as tags, metadata, and nesting. Recognizing this helps the team plan different preparation and analysis work than it would use for fixed-column tables or free-form documents.
Classify JSON and XML exports as unstructured data, and plan only text-mining methods before selecting AI processing.
Explanation
JSON and XML should not be treated as purely unstructured when their tags and nesting provide self-describing organization. Text-mining methods might be useful for text values inside those files, but this guidance ignores structure that can help extraction and analysis.
Overall explanation

Structured data follows a predefined schema, often as rows and columns where each column represents an attribute and each row represents a record. AWS describes structured data as easier to organize, clean, search, and analyze because it follows known rules, as explained in What is Structured Data?.

Semi-structured data sits between tabular structured data and free-form unstructured content. Formats such as JSON and XML do not require a relational table model, but they include self-describing elements such as tags, nesting, and metadata that can guide extraction and processing.

For AI planning, data type affects the work needed before analysis. Fixed-column donation tables are well suited to conventional structured analysis, while narrative PDFs often need preprocessing or AI techniques that can handle unstructured text. AWS Prescriptive Guidance also notes that traditional ML often relies on structured or labeled datasets, while generative AI can learn from large amounts of unstructured or semi-structured data, as described in Differences between traditional AI and generative AI data strategies.

Question 10Skipped

An airline's customer operations team pilots an AI-assisted system that prioritizes refund requests for agent review. The pilot uses requests submitted through the airline's website and shows similar processing times across customer groups. The team plans to expand the system to mobile app requests and call center transcripts, where customers provide information in different formats. Leaders want to reduce the risk that fairness changes after the expansion.

Which governance practice will BEST address this risk?
Correct answer
Monitor prioritization results by customer segment and request channel. Investigate recurring gaps in service outcomes.
Explanation
This practice recognizes that bias can emerge during deployment when the user population, channel, or input format changes. Monitoring by both customer segment and request channel helps leaders detect bias drift that might not appear in overall service metrics.
Remove age-related fields from request records. Use the same prioritization threshold for every request channel.
Explanation
Removing obvious sensitive fields does not prevent other fields or channel patterns from acting as proxies. A single threshold can still produce uneven outcomes if the information quality differs across request channels.
Approve the expansion based on the pilot fairness review. Reassess the system only when complaint volume increases.
Explanation
A pilot review is a useful baseline, but it does not prove that fairness will remain stable after deployment conditions change. Waiting for complaints is reactive and can miss affected customers who do not file complaints.
Retrain the system on all new channel data each month. Compare only aggregate refund processing speed.
Explanation
Retraining on new data can incorporate changing patterns, but it does not automatically prevent unfair outcomes. Aggregate processing speed can remain acceptable while specific customer groups or channels experience worse service.
Overall explanation

Bias risk is not limited to the original training data or the prelaunch test. It can appear at many points in the AI lifecycle, including deployment, when the system begins receiving requests from new channels, new customer groups, or less structured inputs.

In this scenario, mobile app requests and call center transcripts can differ from website forms in wording, completeness, and customer demographics. Ongoing monitoring by customer segment and request channel gives leaders a practical way to detect bias drift, which is a change in fairness or outcome patterns after launch.

A one-time fairness review is not enough because the operating environment can change after a pilot. Removing sensitive attributes is also insufficient because proxy variables, data quality differences, and channel-specific behavior can still create uneven outcomes. Aggregate metrics are useful for business performance, but they can hide subgroup-level harm.

A responsible AI governance program should define fairness indicators, review them regularly, and investigate meaningful differences before they become widespread. For more on responsible AI considerations in AWS guidance, see the Responsible AI section of the AWS Well-Architected Machine Learning Lens.

Question 12Skipped

A specialty coffee roaster's wholesale sales team uses an AI system that assigns each café account a score for the likelihood of placing a repeat order next month. Sales managers want to use the scores to prioritize outreach. Some executives believe the scores prove exactly which accounts will reorder.

Which guidance MOST accurately explains how the sales team should interpret the AI output?
Use each score as a causal explanation that identifies why the account will reorder next month.
Explanation
This guidance confuses prediction with causation or explanation. A score can indicate likelihood, but it does not necessarily explain the underlying business reasons for an account's future behavior.
Treat each score as a deterministic rule that guarantees the account outcome.
Explanation
This guidance incorrectly treats a prediction as a guaranteed result. AI systems can estimate likely outcomes, but real customer behavior can still differ from the predicted score.
Correct answer
Treat each score as a probability-based estimate that supports outreach prioritization.
Explanation
This guidance correctly frames an AI prediction as an estimate based on patterns learned from data. The score can help the sales team rank accounts for outreach, but the score does not prove a future outcome with certainty.
Replace each score with a manual review before any sales outreach can begin.
Explanation
This guidance dismisses the value of prediction for decision support. Human judgment can be useful, especially for high-value accounts, but requiring manual review before any outreach removes the prioritization benefit of the AI system.
Overall explanation

An AI prediction is usually a best estimate about a likely outcome, not a guarantee that the outcome will occur. In this scenario, the score helps the sales team decide where to focus attention, but it should be treated as decision support rather than as a certain statement about the future.

The practical business tradeoff is that prediction scores can improve prioritization at scale, but they can be wrong because customer behavior changes and the model only reflects patterns present in the data it learned from. Leaders should therefore define how the scores will be used, such as outreach thresholds, review for important accounts, and feedback loops, without assuming the system has perfect knowledge.

A deterministic rule, a causal explanation, and a mandatory manual replacement each misunderstand a different part of AI literacy. The AWS Machine Learning Lens describes machine learning workloads in terms of using data and learned patterns to support business outcomes, which is the right mental model for prediction-based use cases. This decision prioritizes the pilot that has demonstrated business value, operational feasibility, user adoption, and strategic alignment. Holding the other pilots avoids scaling initiatives that have unresolved unit economics, manual data upkeep, or unclear decision impact.

Description

This course contains the use of artificial intelligence.

AI is becoming a core business leadership skill. The AWS Certified AI Business Strategist (AIB-C01) certification gives you a way to prove you can turn AI capabilities into real business results.

But this exam is not about memorizing AWS services or writing code. It tests whether you can make good decisions about AI strategy, business value, responsible AI, governance, organizational readiness, and enterprise adoption.

These 3 full-length AIB-C01 practice exams are designed to help you find out whether you're actually ready before you sit for the real exam.

We recommend studying AWS's preparation materials at AWS Skill Builder and the official exam guide prior to this exam.

What you'll get

    3 full-length AWS Certified AI Business Strategist practice exams

    Questions carefully aligned with the official AIB-C01 exam guide

    Coverage of the exam's domains, tasks, and skills

    Detailed explanations for every answer

    Explanations of why the incorrect choices are wrong

    Links to relevant AWS documentation and other authoritative resources

    Realistic business scenarios that test judgment, not trivia

The official exam is weighted across four major areas:

    AI Fundamentals and Literacy - 24%

    AI Strategy and Business Value Creation - 28%

    AI Governance and Responsible AI Leadership - 24%

    Business Readiness, Leadership, and AI Transformation - 24%

These practice exams follow that same structure.

You'll practice topics including generative AI, AI agents, prompt engineering, RAG, AI use-case selection, build-vs-buy decisions, ROI and KPIs, responsible AI, governance, regulatory risk, hallucinations, bias, data readiness, change management, AI maturity, and scaling AI initiatives across an enterprise.

You'll also see the AWS technologies that matter for this certification, including Amazon Bedrock, Amazon SageMaker AI, Amazon Quick, AWS Cloud Adoption Framework, AWS Pricing Calculator, AWS Cost Explorer, and AWS Marketplace. The emphasis is on understanding when and why these services matter to the business, not how to configure them.

Learn from the explanations, not just your score

The point of these tests isn't simply to tell you whether you got a question right.

Every question includes a detailed educational explanation so you can understand the reasoning behind the answer and recognize similar situations on the real exam.

If you uncover a weak area, follow the included documentation links, review the topic, and try again.

That's a much better way to prepare than memorizing answers.

Built for the actual AIB-C01 audience

AWS designed this certification for professionals who evaluate, champion, or scale AI initiatives, including product managers, program managers, consultants, marketers, business analysts, sales professionals, and business leaders. No coding or hands-on AWS implementation experience is required.

These practice exams reflect that.

Expect questions like:

    Is AI actually the right solution for this business problem?

    Which AI initiative should the company prioritize?

    How should success and ROI be measured?

    What governance or human oversight is required?

    Should the organization build, buy, or partner?

    What needs to change before an AI pilot can scale across the enterprise?
