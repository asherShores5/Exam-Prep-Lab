Question 1Skipped

A specialty chemical manufacturer introduced an AI tool that recommends production schedule changes to reduce late orders. The pilot improved on-time delivery at one plant, and executives now want to determine whether the initiative continues to create value as it expands to more plants. Which approach should the executives use?
Correct answer
Set baseline business KPIs, gather user feedback, and review outcomes on a recurring cadence.
Explanation
This approach creates a continuous feedback loop that connects AI performance to business value. Baseline KPIs show whether the initiative improves outcomes over time, and recurring reviews help leaders adjust the initiative as conditions change.
Run a year-end benefits review, and adjust the roadmap after finance validates total savings.
Explanation
A year-end review can confirm financial impact after the fact, but it is not a continuous feedback mechanism. Waiting until the end of the year delays learning and can allow adoption, quality, or value issues to persist.
Track model accuracy and recommendation volume, and approve expansion when technical metrics improve.
Explanation
Technical metrics can help teams understand whether the AI system is functioning, but they do not prove business value. Recommendation volume can increase even when users do not trust or act on the recommendations.
Set adoption targets for each plant, and reward teams that increase AI usage the fastest.
Explanation
Usage targets can encourage adoption, but usage alone is not the same as measurable business value. Rewarding fast usage can create an incentive to use the tool even when recommendations do not improve operational outcomes.
Overall explanation

AI transformation should be managed as an ongoing value cycle, not as a one-time deployment decision. The strongest approach is to establish baseline business KPIs, collect feedback from the people affected by the AI system, and review results regularly so leaders can decide whether to scale, revise, or stop the initiative.

For a production scheduling use case, useful business measures could include on-time delivery, schedule stability, planner productivity, and exception rates. User feedback is equally important because the system can appear successful in reports while planners find the recommendations hard to trust or difficult to use in daily workflows.

Technical measures such as accuracy and recommendation volume are useful supporting indicators, but they are not sufficient success metrics for business transformation. Annual reviews and adoption-only targets are common anti-patterns because they either delay learning or optimize for activity rather than impact. AWS guidance for well-architected machine learning workloads emphasizes aligning ML systems with business goals and operating them with feedback and continuous improvement practices. Learn more in the AWS Well-Architected Machine Learning Lens.

Question 2Skipped

A professional sports league's analytics team is planning AI projects from several data sources. The sources include a player statistics spreadsheet with fixed columns for player ID, game date, minutes played, and points scored; scanned scouting reports and game photos; and JSON exports from a fan engagement platform. Leaders want to classify the data types correctly before choosing AI approaches. Which TWO actions should the team take?
Correct selection
Use the player statistics spreadsheet as structured data because fixed columns define consistent record attributes.
Explanation
This option is correct because the spreadsheet has a standardized format with predefined columns such as player ID, game date, minutes played, and points scored. Structured data is typically tabular, with rows representing records and columns representing attributes.
Require explicit labels for any generative AI use of the reports and photos before initial pre-training.
Explanation
This option is incorrect because generative AI models can learn from large volumes of unstructured or semi-structured data without explicit labels for pre-training. Labels are especially important for supervised machine learning, where the model learns from examples tied to known outcomes.
Correct selection
Plan preprocessing and AI techniques for scanned reports and photos because they are unstructured content.
Explanation
This option is correct because scanned reports and photos are examples of unstructured data that do not naturally fit a predefined tabular schema. These sources often need preprocessing or AI techniques such as text extraction, natural language processing, or image recognition before they can support analysis.
Classify the JSON fan export as fully structured data because keys and nesting make every field relational.
Explanation
This option is incorrect because JSON is commonly considered semi-structured data, not fully structured relational data. Keys and nesting provide self-describing elements, but they do not make the data equivalent to a fixed table with predefined rows and columns.
Convert all scanned reports into spreadsheet rows before deciding whether AI can search or analyze them.
Explanation
This option is incorrect because unstructured data does not always need to be converted into spreadsheet rows before AI can use it. AI and ML techniques can work with text, images, and scanned documents after appropriate preprocessing for the intended use case.
Overall explanation

Structured data follows a standardized format or predefined schema, so the player statistics spreadsheet is a good fit for tabular analysis because each column represents a consistent attribute. AWS describes structured data as data with defined field names, data types, and constraints, and commonly associates it with rows and columns that are easier to organize, clean, search, and analyze. See What is structured data? and Structured data vs. unstructured data.

Unstructured data, such as scanned documents and images, is not forced into a predefined schema. That does not make it unusable for AI, but it often changes the approach: teams may need preprocessing and AI techniques such as natural language processing, text mining, image recognition, or similar methods to extract useful signals. JSON exports are better described as semi-structured because they use self-describing elements such as keys, metadata, and nesting without necessarily following a relational table model.

The data type also affects strategic AI planning. Traditional supervised ML often depends on structured or labeled datasets, while generative AI can learn from large amounts of unstructured or semi-structured data without explicit labels for pre-training, as described in Differences between traditional AI and generative AI data needs. For business leaders, the practical lesson is to match the AI initiative to the data source instead of assuming that every useful source must first become a spreadsheet or that every AI use case requires labels.

Question 3Skipped

A continuing education nonprofit has completed an AI pilot that matches adult learners with training programs. The pilot reduced counselor research time in one city. Executives want to scale AI across services while showing value in the next quarter and building toward enterprise-wide adoption.

Which approach should the nonprofit take?
Fund separate technical proofs of concept. Rank teams by model novelty. Decide rollout priorities after experiments finish.
Explanation
This approach emphasizes technical experimentation before business value. AWS guidance warns that technical proofs of concept can fail to progress when they are not tied to tangible business and customer outcomes.
Correct answer
Set a business-focused north star. Select adjacent use cases with measurable value. Use each win to guide rollout.
Explanation
This approach connects near-term results to a broader enterprise direction. It helps the nonprofit build confidence from measurable outcomes while avoiding a collection of disconnected experiments.
Delay new use cases until an enterprise AI office is fully operating. Use governance maturity as the main scale criterion.
Explanation
This approach makes organizational structure the gating factor for all progress. Governance and operating models matter, but the scenario requires continued momentum and measurable business value while scaling.
Mandate the pilot across every program. Keep the original workflow unchanged. Measure adoption after rollout completes.
Explanation
This approach treats the first pilot as a universal solution without validating fit across different services. Scaling by mandate can create resistance and can miss opportunities to learn from each expansion step.
Overall explanation

Effective AI scaling is a stepwise business-value journey, not a choice between one isolated pilot and an immediate enterprise rollout. AWS CAF-AI positions AI transformation as an ongoing journey that helps organizations move beyond a single proof of concept and identify near-term improvement areas. See AWS Cloud Adoption Framework for Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, and Generative AI.

The best approach is to define a business and customer centered north star, then choose use cases that can produce measurable results while contributing to the larger portfolio. This balances near-term impact with longer-term enterprise ambition, which is especially important when leaders need evidence before expanding investment. AWS describes this balance in Business perspective: The AI strategy in the age of AI/ML.

Isolated technical proofs of concept are a common anti-pattern because they can demonstrate interesting capabilities without creating momentum toward business outcomes. A forced broad rollout has the opposite problem, because it assumes the first use case fits every context without staged learning. Delaying all progress for a fully mature operating model also slows value creation when the organization can instead scale through governed, measurable increments.

Question 4Skipped

A regional news publisher's digital products team is evaluating generative AI features to summarize articles and draft subscriber newsletters. Early pilots used the most capable foundation model for every task and gave all editorial staff unrestricted access, which caused unpredictable monthly costs. Leaders want to scale only if the team can forecast spend, control usage, and keep costs aligned with business value.

Which TWO actions should the team take?
Correct selection
Match each task to the lowest-cost model that satisfies quality requirements.
Explanation
Different foundation models can have different prices and capabilities, so using the same high-capability model for every task can waste budget. Matching model capability to the business need is a basic optimization strategy that preserves acceptable output quality while reducing unnecessary cost.
Use one premium model for every workflow to simplify governance and adoption.
Explanation
Using one premium model for all workflows is a technology standardization shortcut, not a cost control. It can make simple tasks more expensive than necessary and does not address usage forecasting or value-based scaling.
Correct selection
Set usage budgets and track cost per workflow against defined business outcomes.
Explanation
Usage budgets and workflow-level cost tracking create a practical cost planning baseline. Connecting spend to business outcomes helps leaders compare variable AI costs with measurable value before broader rollout.
Expand access to all employees before measuring adoption and unit economics.
Explanation
Broad access before measurement can increase consumption without showing whether the use cases justify the spend. This approach is an adoption-first anti-pattern because it scales cost before validating demand and business value.
Customize a model for each use case before confirming recurring business value.
Explanation
Customization can be appropriate in some situations, but it should be justified by clear business value and quality requirements. Applying customization to every use case at the start adds cost and complexity before the publisher knows which workflows should scale.
Overall explanation

Effective AI cost control starts with planning, measurement, and fit-for-purpose model selection. Generative AI costs can vary based on the model used, the amount of usage, and the type of request, so teams should avoid treating AI spend as a fixed one-time expense.

Budgets and cost tracking help leaders create guardrails before adoption expands. AWS Budgets supports planning and monitoring spend, which aligns with the business need to forecast and control AI costs rather than discovering overruns after usage grows. See Managing your costs with AWS Budgets.

Model choice is also a business decision. A higher-capability foundation model can be valuable for complex work, but simpler workflows such as short summaries or draft newsletters might meet requirements with a lower-cost model. Amazon Bedrock supports access to multiple foundation models, and model evaluation helps teams compare quality for a use case before standardizing on an approach. See What is Amazon Bedrock? and Evaluate the performance of Amazon Bedrock resources.

The incorrect approaches scale technology before proving value. Using one premium model, opening access broadly, or customizing every use case can all increase spend without demonstrating that the incremental cost improves business outcomes.

Question 5Skipped

A mid-sized commercial law firm is losing corporate clients to larger firms that use AI for contract review, matter intake, and knowledge search. The firm's partners agree that AI could affect client retention, but the firm has limited AI experience and uneven data practices across practice groups. The partners want an investment approach that reflects competitive pressure without creating an unfocused spending program.

Which funding approach should the firm choose?
Correct answer
Fund a focused portfolio of outcome-linked pilots while investing in data, governance, and skills needed to scale proven use cases.
Explanation
This approach balances competitive urgency with the firm's current maturity. It supports measurable business outcomes first, while also building the foundational capabilities needed to move successful pilots toward broader value.
Buy a general-purpose AI platform first, then ask each department to identify ways to use the available capabilities.
Explanation
This approach starts with technology selection instead of business outcomes. It can create tool adoption activity, but it does not ensure that investment targets the client retention and productivity problems that matter most.
Commit enterprise-wide funding to deploy AI in every practice area and business function to quickly match competitors' visible capabilities.
Explanation
This approach overreacts to competitive pressure by scaling before the firm has proven value or readiness. Broad deployment without clear outcomes can waste investment and increase adoption, governance, and data quality risks.
Limit funding to informal staff experiments until the market stabilizes and competitors prove which tools remain viable.
Explanation
This approach underinvests relative to the competitive threat that is already affecting client retention. Informal experiments can generate learning, but they do not create a disciplined path to business value or scalable adoption.
Overall explanation

A practical AI investment level should reflect both external competitive dynamics and internal organizational maturity. When competitors are already using AI to improve customer outcomes, waiting too long can increase strategic risk, but enterprise-wide spending can also fail if the organization has not identified measurable opportunities or built supporting capabilities.

AWS CAF-AI describes AI adoption as a journey for creating business value from AI, ML, and generative AI, with foundational capabilities growing as maturity increases. The guidance emphasizes working backward from expected business outcomes, transformation needs, stakeholders, measurable results, and required data assets rather than beginning with broad technology enthusiasm. See AWS Cloud Adoption Framework for Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, and Generative AI and AI cloud transformation value chain.

The right investment stance is neither passive experimentation nor an immediate enterprise-wide rollout. A focused portfolio of outcome-linked pilots lets leaders test value in areas that matter, such as client retention or productivity, while targeted investments in data, governance, and skills prepare successful initiatives to move from pilot to sustained scale.

Question 6Skipped

A renewable energy retailer's executive team has many ideas for generative AI, including customer email drafting, contract review, outage communications, and sales coaching. The organization has no production AI solutions, uneven access to reliable data, and limited AI governance. Leaders want to decide where to invest next quarter so the organization can move from early exploration toward practical experimentation while staying aligned to business goals.

Which action should the executive team take FIRST?
Assign one overall maturity rating to the organization, then prioritize only the teams with the highest technical readiness.
Explanation
A single maturity label can hide differences across business, people, governance, platform, security, and operations dimensions. Prioritizing only technical readiness also ignores business impact and strategic alignment.
Correct answer
Create a use case matrix that ranks candidate AI initiatives by feasibility, business impact, and strategic alignment.
Explanation
This action fits an early maturity stage because it turns a broad set of ideas into a prioritized shortlist for proofs of concept. It connects investment decisions to business goals while considering whether the organization is ready to pursue each opportunity.
Select the AI tool with the fastest vendor demo, then require each department to submit a pilot for the tool.
Explanation
Choosing a tool first is a technology-led approach that can create pilots without clear business value. Department-wide submissions can increase activity, but they do not provide a disciplined progression path based on maturity and objectives.
Fund a broad production rollout of the most popular AI request, then collect adoption data to justify future investment.
Explanation
A broad production rollout is premature when the organization has no production AI experience and limited governance. Popularity alone does not show feasibility, data readiness, or strategic value.
Overall explanation

For an organization at an early stage of generative AI adoption, investment prioritization should begin with structured discovery and readiness building, not with a production rollout or a tool mandate. AWS describes Level 1, Envision, as a stage for building awareness, exploring use cases, mapping initial use cases to business objectives, and identifying high-value data sources.

The practical progression from Envision to Experiment is to create a use case matrix that evaluates opportunities by feasibility, business impact, and alignment with strategic goals. This approach creates a shortlist of proof-of-concept candidates that can test value without overcommitting resources before the organization has validated governance, data, and operating practices.

A maturity assessment should not be reduced to tool readiness or one blanket label for the whole enterprise. AWS frames generative AI maturity across multiple adoption pillars, including Business, People, Governance, Platform, Security, and Operations, so leaders should consider both business value and readiness constraints when sequencing investments.

Learn more in the AWS Prescriptive Guidance pages for the Level 1: Envision maturity level, the generative AI maturity model aspects, and the AWS CAF-AI guidance on AI strategy in the age of AI and ML.

Question 7Skipped

A public water utility's billing operations group is deciding whether to use AI to process customer billing exception requests. The requests are governed by fixed tariff rules, the decisions must be consistent for the same facts, and staff can resolve most cases by following a simple workflow. The group has a small set of historical examples, and an offline proof of concept did not improve results enough to justify expected ongoing costs. Which TWO recommendations should the group make?
Expand the PoC and count only model usage charges while excluding ongoing support costs.
Explanation
This recommendation uses an incomplete financial comparison. AWS guidance says cost evaluation should include the full ML lifecycle, including data work, training, hosting, inference, retraining, monitoring, and maintenance.
Launch an AI solution because automation can reduce review time when documented policies exist.
Explanation
This recommendation treats AI as the default path because automation is possible. The scenario describes fixed policy decisions where a simpler approach can be more effective and less costly than AI.
Correct selection
Pause AI investment and revisit after more relevant data and value evidence are available.
Explanation
This recommendation fits the weak PoC result and the limited historical data. AWS guidance supports moving on, reframing the problem, or collecting more data when a PoC does not show that the required business value can be achieved.
Correct selection
Use deterministic decision rules and streamline exception handling for the tariff scenarios.
Explanation
This recommendation fits a problem that is governed by fixed rules and requires consistent outcomes. AWS guidance states that some business problems do not need ML because simple business rules can do a better job.
Replace the current workflow with generative AI and adjust prompts until outputs seem consistent.
Explanation
This recommendation introduces generative AI into a task that requires rule-based consistency. Prompt adjustment does not address the lack of demonstrated business value, limited data, or the availability of a simpler baseline.
Overall explanation

AI is not the best answer simply because a process is manual or time-consuming. For problems controlled by clear business rules, a deterministic rules-based approach can be easier to govern, cheaper to operate, and more consistent than an ML or generative AI solution.

AWS frames AI and ML adoption as a business-value decision. The AWS CAF Business Perspective recommends defining the business problem, identifying a measurable performance metric, and testing whether the problem can be modeled with available data. It also states that some business problems do not need ML because simple rules may work better, and other problems may not have enough data for ML to be appropriate: AWS CAF Business Perspective, Data Science.

The weak proof of concept is an important stop signal, not merely a reason to keep experimenting. AWS guidance supports using offline data to test whether the required business value can be achieved, then reframing the problem, collecting more data, or moving on if results are not satisfactory. The AWS Machine Learning Lens also warns against jumping directly to ML, underestimating total cost, and failing to compare against simpler baselines: Machine Learning Lens, Evaluate the cost of machine learning.

Question 8Skipped

A private university's bursar office receives thousands of student requests to reverse late-payment fees each term. The written policy says to reverse a fee only when the payment was posted within 2 calendar days of the deadline and the student has not received a reversal during the academic year. Staff members want faster turnaround, consistent decisions, and clear explanations for denials.

Which approach should the office use?
Train a predictive model that learns waiver decisions from prior cases and auto-approves matching requests.
Explanation
This uses ML for a decision that the policy already defines. ML would produce probabilistic predictions based on past examples, which is unnecessary when simple programmable rules can determine eligibility.
Use SageMaker Canvas to classify requests from historical payment records and denial notes.
Explanation
SageMaker Canvas is a no-code ML approach, so it still relies on data, models, and evaluation. No-code ML does not change the fit analysis when a simple published policy is enough.
Correct answer
Implement a deterministic workflow that applies the published policy and routes unclear requests to staff.
Explanation
This is correct because the decision is governed by explicit eligibility criteria that can be evaluated with rules. A rules workflow also supports consistent outcomes and straightforward explanations while leaving edge cases for humans.
Deploy a generative AI assistant that summarizes each request and recommends a waiver decision.
Explanation
Summarization may help staff read long requests, but it does not directly enforce the policy. A recommendation system also introduces probabilistic judgment where the organization needs clear, consistent fee decisions.
Overall explanation

For this scenario, the important distinction is deterministic rules versus probabilistic learning. AWS guidance says ML is not needed when a target value can be determined by programmable rules or predetermined steps without data-driven learning: When to use machine learning. The bursar's policy has a small set of explicit conditions, so an ordinary workflow can apply those conditions consistently and can explain a denial by pointing to the unmet condition.

ML and broader AI are better fits when the rules are hard for humans to write reliably, many factors interact, or human judgment cannot scale. AWS describes ML decisions as probabilistic rather than deterministic, so using a model would shift the decision into a likelihood-based framing that is not needed for a fixed policy: What is machine learning?. No-code tooling such as Amazon SageMaker Canvas can reduce implementation effort, but it remains an ML approach and should be chosen only when the business problem needs learning from data, as described on Amazon SageMaker Canvas.

Question 9Skipped

A global apparel brand's merchandising, marketing, store operations, and customer service teams have begun using AI informally. Executives want employees across the enterprise to understand AI opportunities, limitations, and safe usage before the company scales additional AI initiatives. The company also wants learning activities to produce practical ideas connected to business priorities rather than isolated experiments. Which workforce development approach should the company take?
Train only analytics specialists on AI concepts, advanced tooling, and vendor certification paths before involving business teams.
Explanation
Specialist training can improve technical capability, but it does not address the need for AI literacy across business functions. Enterprise adoption depends on leaders and nontechnical employees understanding opportunities, limitations, and safe usage.
Correct answer
Create role-based AI education, responsible use guidance, and guided business-case pilots for cross-functional teams.
Explanation
This approach builds enterprise-wide AI literacy while recognizing that different roles need different levels of depth. Responsible use guidance sets safe boundaries, and guided pilots help employees apply learning to business priorities.
Give all employees access to popular AI tools, track adoption metrics, and reward the teams with highest usage.
Explanation
Broad access can increase experimentation, but access alone does not create shared understanding or responsible usage habits. Rewarding high usage can encourage activity without proving business value, risk awareness, or quality of outcomes.
Run open-ended innovation contests, defer usage guidance, and fund proposals based on employee enthusiasm across departments.
Explanation
Innovation contests can generate ideas, but deferring guidance creates unmanaged risk and inconsistent practices. Funding ideas mainly because of enthusiasm can favor novelty over responsible, feasible, and business-aligned outcomes.
Overall explanation

For enterprise AI transformation, workforce development should combine role-based literacy, responsible use expectations, and applied experiences tied to business outcomes. A marketer, store manager, legal reviewer, and contact center leader do not need the same technical depth, but they all need a common vocabulary about AI benefits, limitations, data sensitivity, bias, human oversight, and escalation paths.

Guided pilots, POCs, or hackathons work best when they are framed as learning mechanisms and business discovery tools, not as ungoverned tool trials. This approach helps employees practice identifying use cases, testing assumptions, and recognizing risk before an initiative receives larger funding.

Giving broad tool access or rewarding high usage is a common technology-first anti-pattern because adoption metrics can rise without improving judgment or business value. Training only technical specialists or forcing every employee through identical deep technical courses also misses the goal of enterprise-wide literacy, which is shared readiness across roles. AWS guidance on cloud adoption emphasizes organizational change and people capabilities, and responsible AI guidance highlights practices such as transparency, fairness, and human-centered oversight. See AWS Cloud Adoption Framework: People perspective and Responsible AI in the Machine Learning Lens.

Question 10Skipped

A city museum's visitor experience team is testing a generative AI assistant to sort open-ended survey comments into routing categories for follow-up. The current prompt says, "Review this comment and tell us what it is about." The assistant often returns paragraphs, invents new category names, or gives multiple categories when the team needs one of the approved labels.

Which change should the team make to the prompt?
Correct answer
Provide routing context, list the approved feedback categories, include the comment, and ask for one category at the end.
Explanation
This option applies basic prompt engineering principles by making the task clear, reducing ambiguity, and naming the allowed classification choices. Asking for one category at the end helps guide the model toward the desired output format instead of a free-form response.
Ask the model to infer the best category, include the comment, and request a short explanation before the category.
Explanation
Asking the model to infer categories does not solve the problem of invented or inconsistent labels. Requesting an explanation before the category also works against the team's need for a single approved routing label.
Fine-tune the model on past comments, remove category names, and ask the model to learn the routing pattern.
Explanation
Fine-tuning is not the same as prompt engineering, and the scenario asks for a prompt change. Removing the category names would make the classification task less explicit and could increase inconsistent outputs.
Use a longer prompt with broad museum background, place the question first, and ask for any useful category or summary.
Explanation
Adding broad background can make a prompt longer without making the task clearer. Asking for any useful category or summary preserves the ambiguity that caused free-form responses and invented labels.
Overall explanation

Prompt engineering is about shaping the input to an LLM so the model has a clear task, useful context, and enough structure to produce a relevant response. AWS describes a prompt as user-provided input that guides an LLM, and notes that prompt quality can materially affect output quality.

For a classification task, the prompt should reduce ambiguity by naming the approved answer choices and clearly asking the model to choose from them. AWS guidance also recommends clear, complete instructions and placing the question or instruction at the end of the prompt for better results, which fits the museum team's need for one routing label.

Fine-tuning, broad background text, or open-ended inference can be reasonable in other situations, but they do not directly address the prompt design issue here. A better prompt does not guarantee identical outputs every time, because LLM generation can vary, but clearer instructions and explicit categories improve the chance of getting the desired format. For more detail, see What is prompt engineering?, Prompt engineering guidelines, and Design a prompt.

Question 11Skipped

A public university's scholarship office pilots an AI tool that ranks scholarship applications for committee review. During the pilot, advisors notice that applicants from rural high schools receive lower rankings than comparable applicants from urban high schools. University leaders want to continue the pilot only if the team addresses the responsible AI concern most directly related to unequal outcomes across applicant groups.

Which action should the scholarship office take?
Limit access to application data. Retain only the information needed for scholarship decisions.
Explanation
This action supports privacy by reducing unnecessary data exposure and limiting access to sensitive information. Privacy controls are important, but they do not directly determine whether ranking outcomes are equitable across applicant groups.
Document the tool's purpose, limitations, and escalation path. Share review procedures with students and staff.
Explanation
This action supports transparency by helping stakeholders understand how the tool is used and how concerns can be raised. Transparency does not by itself detect or correct unequal ranking outcomes across applicant groups.
Correct answer
Evaluate ranking outcomes across applicant segments. Adjust the selection process to reduce unfair disparities.
Explanation
This action directly addresses the risk that the AI tool is producing systematically different outcomes for groups of applicants. Evaluating outcomes by applicant segment and correcting unfair disparities applies the responsible AI principle of fairness.
Test the tool with new application cycles. Confirm rankings stay consistent when inputs change.
Explanation
This action supports robustness by checking whether the tool behaves reliably as conditions change. Robustness testing can reveal instability, but it does not directly address whether outcomes are unfair for a specific applicant group.
Overall explanation

Fairness focuses on whether an AI system produces equitable treatment and outcomes for affected groups. In this scenario, the issue is not primarily whether the tool is documented, secure, or stable. The concern is that similarly qualified applicants appear to receive different rankings based on a group characteristic that may reflect unequal opportunity or biased data patterns.

A responsible AI approach does not require abandoning the pilot immediately, but it does require governance before broader use. The scholarship office should examine outcomes across relevant applicant segments, identify sources of disparity, and adjust the human review or selection process so the tool does not reinforce unfair differences. AWS describes responsible AI as including dimensions such as fairness, explainability, privacy and security, safety, transparency, and robustness in the Responsible AI section of the AWS Well-Architected Machine Learning Lens.

The other actions map to valid responsible AI dimensions, but they do not address the stated risk as directly. Privacy controls reduce inappropriate data use, transparency helps stakeholders understand the system, and robustness testing checks reliability under changing conditions. None of those actions, by themselves, determines whether rural and urban applicants are being treated fairly by the ranking process.

Question 12Skipped

A public university's scholarship office uses an AI-assisted system to prioritize outreach to prospective students. An initial review shows balanced outreach recommendations across applicant groups. Over the next admissions cycles, the mix of applicants, majors, and scholarship criteria changes. Leaders want governance practices that account for bias risks across the AI lifecycle and after launch.

Which TWO actions should the scholarship office take?
Exclude demographic attributes from governance reviews and stop comparing outcomes across applicant groups.
Explanation
Removing demographic attributes from review activities does not guarantee fair outcomes because other variables can act as proxies. It can also make it harder to measure whether recommendations affect groups differently, subject to the university's legal and privacy requirements.
Limit governance checks to final reports and skip earlier data, labeling, and design choices.
Explanation
Final output reviews can identify some symptoms of bias, but they do not reveal all upstream causes. Bias can arise during problem framing, data collection, labeling, evaluation, and feedback after launch.
Correct selection
Monitor recommendation outcomes by applicant group after launch and review trend changes regularly.
Explanation
A fairness review at launch is only a point-in-time assessment. Applicant populations and decision criteria can change, so ongoing outcome monitoring helps detect bias drift after deployment.
Correct selection
Review data sources and labeling practices for representation gaps before major model updates.
Explanation
Bias can enter before a model is used through incomplete source data, inconsistent labels, or definitions that disadvantage some groups. Reviewing these inputs before major updates helps the office identify bias risks earlier in the lifecycle.
Approve the system indefinitely after the first fairness review shows balanced recommendation results.
Explanation
A one-time approval does not address changes in data, users, or institutional priorities over time. This approach is a governance anti-pattern because it treats fairness as static instead of something that must be reassessed.
Overall explanation

Bias can appear at multiple points in the AI lifecycle, including problem definition, data selection, labeling, model evaluation, deployment, and user feedback. A balanced launch review is useful, but it does not prove that the system will remain fair when the applicant population, scholarship rules, or institutional priorities change.

Bias drift occurs when the fairness characteristics of an AI system change over time. For a scholarship outreach system, a shift in applicant demographics or program demand can cause recommendation patterns that were previously balanced to become uneven, even if the system itself has not been intentionally changed.

Good responsible AI leadership combines preventive reviews, such as checking data sources and labeling practices, with ongoing monitoring of outcomes across relevant groups. AWS guidance on responsible AI emphasizes governance, transparency, and continuous evaluation as part of building and operating AI systems responsibly. For more context, see the Responsible AI section of the AWS Well-Architected Machine Learning Lens.

Question 13Skipped

A nonprofit legal aid network is running a generative AI proof of concept (PoC) to help caseworkers draft intake summaries from client interviews. Leaders will decide whether to fund a broader rollout after 6 weeks. They want leading indicators that show the project is likely to create business value, not merely that the demo works. Which action should the leaders take?
Use real client records by default, prioritize fine-tuning, and judge success by more fluent summary language in drafts.
Explanation
Sensitive real data should not be the default for a PoC; synthetic or fully anonymized data is preferred unless proper approvals are obtained. Fluency alone does not validate business value, and prioritizing fine-tuning by default can add complexity before proving that a simpler approach is sufficient.
Correct answer
Track task completion, draft usability, data readiness, SME feedback, and per-summary cost against exit thresholds.
Explanation
This action uses leading indicators across business value, user usefulness, data readiness, stakeholder validation, and economics. Clear exit thresholds help leaders make an evidence-based rollout decision rather than relying on enthusiasm for a working demo.
Expand testing to all offices, collect adoption counts, and resolve privacy concerns after usage patterns stabilize.
Explanation
Expanding broadly before addressing privacy and risk is not a sound PoC success strategy. Adoption counts can indicate interest, but they do not prove that outputs are useful, data is ready, or the solution is financially viable at scale.

Explanation
Vendor maturity and model size do not reliably capture the responsible AI risk of a specific business use case. Deferring safeguards until launch is also too late because risk rankings should help incorporate controls during design and throughout the lifecycle.
Correct answer
Score each use case by likelihood and severity, maintain a risk registry, and add oversight for consequential decisions.
Explanation
This approach classifies risk at the use-case level and prioritizes governance based on both likelihood and impact severity. A risk registry supports lifecycle tracking, and employment-related decisions can require additional safeguards because they affect consequential outcomes.
Apply the Responsible AI Lens as a legal checklist, assign fixed controls, and classify compliance risk without counsel review.
Explanation
The Responsible AI Lens provides advisory guidance and considerations, not a legal compliance checklist. Organizations still need appropriate legal or compliance review for obligations that apply to their use cases.
Overall explanation

Responsible AI governance works best when risk is assessed for a specific AI use case, not only for a model, vendor, or technology category. The AWS Well-Architected Responsible AI Lens recommends estimating the likelihood and severity of potential harmful interactions, then mapping those estimates to a risk level that can guide mitigation priorities. See the Responsible AI Lens guidance on assessing risks at Assess risks and assigning overall risk levels at Assign an overall risk level.

A risk registry gives leaders a practical way to compare use cases over time, record affected stakeholders, capture high-level mitigations, and update risk decisions as evidence changes. The job candidate screening use case also touches employment outcomes, so it should receive stronger attention to human oversight, testing, and safeguards under the AWS Responsible AI Policy for consequential decisions. Service Cards and similar transparency resources are useful inputs, but they do not replace the organization's responsibility to test the system for its own use case and deployment context. For lifecycle tracking, see Maintain a risk registry and the AWS Responsible AI Policy.
